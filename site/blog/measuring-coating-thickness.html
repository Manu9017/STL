Coating quality

Measuring galvanized coating thickness: gauges, readings and ISO 1461

A gauge reading is easy to take and easy to get wrong. How to check the gauge, where to measure, and how to judge local and mean values against ISO 1461.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 4 min read

Coating thickness is the number every galvanizing order is judged on. A magnetic gauge gives it in seconds, which is exactly why it is so often taken badly. A gauge that was not checked, readings taken on an edge, or an average worked out the wrong way can fail a good load or pass a thin one. Many arguments between a galvanizer and a customer turn out to be about how the readings were taken, not about the coating.

What ISO 1461 actually judges

ISO 1461 sets two minimums for each class of article, and they are judged in different ways:

  • Local coating thickness is the average of the gauge readings taken within one reference area. Every reference area has to meet the local minimum.
  • Mean coating thickness is the average of the local values over one large article, or over all the articles in the control sample. It has to meet the mean minimum, which is the higher of the two.

Single readings are not judged one by one. One low reading inside a reference area that averages above the local minimum is not a failure. This is the most common misunderstanding on the inspection bench, and it works both ways: an area can fail even when no single reading looks alarming.

Article, not centrifugedLocal minimumMean minimum
Steel over 6 mm70 µm (505 g/m²)85 µm (610 g/m²)
Steel over 3 mm up to 6 mm55 µm (395 g/m²)70 µm (505 g/m²)

Centrifuged work, such as bolts and small parts from a spin line, has its own lower table in the same standard. The g/m² figures use the standard's nominal 7.2 g/m² for each micrometre.

Check the gauge before you trust it

Magnetic gauges, described in ISO 2178, measure the distance between the probe and the steel underneath. They do not know what the coating is made of, and they drift.

  • Zero the gauge on uncoated steel like the work: similar thickness, curvature and surface. A zero set on a thick block reads differently on thin sheet.
  • Check it on calibration foils that bracket the thickness you expect, one thinner and one thicker than the coating.
  • Check again during the shift, after the gauge is dropped, and whenever the probe is changed. Write the check down.
  • Keep the probe tip clean. Zinc dust and grit on the tip add to every reading.

Where to take the readings

  • Stay away from edges, corners, holes and flame-cut faces. The magnetic field is distorted there, and the coating is often different anyway.
  • Spread the reference areas over the article: both faces of plates and flanges, the web of a beam, inside and outside an angle. The coating varies between them, and the customer is buying all of it.
  • On small or strongly curved parts use a probe made for curved surfaces, or weigh the coating instead.
  • Do not measure on drips, runs or lumps; they are not the coating the standard is talking about.
  • Measure once the work has cooled, and before it is painted or packed.

Why one part of an article is thicker than another

  • Steel chemistry. Silicon and phosphorus speed up the reaction between iron and zinc, so reactive steel grows thicker, often duller coatings. See silicon and the Sandelin effect.
  • Section thickness. Heavy sections stay hot longer after they leave the zinc, so the alloy layers keep growing until the work is quenched.
  • Surface roughness. Blast-cleaned or over-pickled steel takes a thicker coating than smooth steel.
  • Drainage. Zinc runs off top surfaces and gathers on lower edges and in corners as the work is withdrawn.
  • Welds. Weld metal has its own chemistry and can coat thicker and darker than the steel beside it.

None of these is a defect while every reference area meets the local minimum and the mean meets the mean minimum.

When the gauge and the customer disagree

A dispute is settled by a destructive method. In the gravimetric method of ISO 1460, a weighed sample is stripped of its zinc in an inhibited acid and weighed again, and the loss per square metre is the coating mass. In the microscopical method of ISO 1463, a cross-section is cut, polished and measured under a microscope. Both destroy the sample, so they settle arguments rather than replace the gauge.

A coating inspection, step by step

StepWhat to do
1. Agree the standardThe standard and its edition, the article class, and any extra thickness written into the order
2. Check the gaugeZero on bare steel like the work, check on two foils, write it down
3. Take the sampleThe number of articles the standard sets for the size of the lot
4. MeasureA set of readings in each reference area, away from edges, with several areas on each article
5. Work it outThe local value of each area, then the mean over the sample
6. JudgeEvery local value at or above the local minimum; the mean at or above the mean minimum
7. RecordReadings, gauge and foils, who measured, and what was decided
Note

Coating far above the minimum is not free. Every 10 µm extra on steel with 30 m² of surface per tonne is about 2 kg of zinc per tonne. The coating check calculator shows it for your steel.
