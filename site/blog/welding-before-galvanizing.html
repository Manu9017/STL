Welding before galvanizing: slag, spatter and weld metal

Bare spots beside welds, thick dark weld beads and rust weeping from joints all start in the fabrication shop. What to do with welds before steel is galvanized.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 2 min read

Welds are where a large share of galvanizing rejects begin. The galvanizer sees them first, but the causes are in the fabrication shop: slag left on the bead, sprays that keep the zinc off, filler metal that reacts differently from the steel, and joints that trap acid. Each has a simple fix before the steel is sent.

Slag does not pickle off

Manual metal arc and flux-cored welding leave a glassy slag on the bead. It is not metal, so hydrochloric acid does not dissolve it and the degreaser does not touch it, and molten zinc will not wet it. The result is a bare line along the weld. Chip, wire-brush, grind or blast every trace of slag off before the work is sent. Solid-wire MIG and MAG welding leaves far less slag, but even there small glassy islands sit on the bead surface and need brushing.

Spatter and anti-spatter sprays

Spatter balls stuck to the steel get coated with zinc but stay as lumps, which matter on visible or painted work. The sprays used to stop spatter matter more. Silicone-based anti-spatter sprays are not removed by alkaline degreasing or by acid, and leave bare patches wherever they were sprayed. Use silicone-free, water-based products, and keep them away from surfaces that are not near the weld.

Weld metal reacts differently

Filler wires and electrodes carry silicon and manganese to make sound welds, and common solid wires carry well over half a per cent of silicon. In the zinc, silicon speeds up the reaction between iron and zinc, so the weld bead often grows a thicker, darker coating than the plate beside it. It protects just as well, but on architectural work it shows. Where appearance matters, choose a low-silicon filler, and grind visible welds smooth. See silicon and the Sandelin effect.

Joints that trap acid

Where two plates overlap and are only stitch-welded, pretreatment liquids get into the gap and cannot be rinsed out. In the kettle the trapped liquid boils; afterwards it weeps out as rust-brown streaks that no repair stops. Seal-weld overlaps all round, or leave a gap wide enough to clean and drain. A large sealed overlap needs a small vent hole, so that air and moisture inside cannot burst the weld when the work meets the zinc. The same applies to every hollow section and every welded-on cap: see designing steel for galvanizing.

Cracks, notches and undercut

Undercut, unfused roots and notches at the weld toe concentrate stress. In heavy, highly restrained fabrications in high-strength steel, they are where cracking starts during galvanizing. Good welding is part of good galvanizing; see embrittlement and galvanizing.

Pretreatment that cleans what it can

Degreasing, pickling, rinsing and fluxing remove oil, rust and scale. Slag and silicone have to come off in the fabrication shop.
