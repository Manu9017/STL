Is your kettle wearing out? Wall checks and warning signs

Kettles rarely fail without warning. Wall temperatures, dross and fuel trends, wall bulge and the leak pits: what to log every day, and when to plan the change.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

Zinc dissolves the kettle wall all the time. An evenly heated kettle loses steel slowly and lasts for years; a kettle with a hot zone loses it fast in one place. A kettle that fails in service lets molten zinc into the furnace, stops the plant for weeks and puts people at risk. A planned change costs a fraction of that, and the signs that it is due are there to be read.

How a kettle wears

  • General thinning. All the wetted wall loses steel at a slow rate set by the zinc temperature and the steel of the kettle.
  • Local thinning at hot spots. Where a burner jet or hot gas strikes, the wall runs hotter and dissolves much faster. Above about 480 °C the attack speeds up sharply.
  • Thinning behind dross. Dross insulates the wall it sits against; if that part of the wall is heated, it runs hot.
  • Bulging. Hot steel under the weight of the zinc creeps slowly outwards, and thinner walls bulge faster.

What to log every day

  • Wall temperatures in every zone, with the zinc temperature. Note the highest wall reading and the spread between zones. A zone that runs hotter than the others for weeks is wearing faster.
  • Firing: burner load or fuel used, and fuel per tonne. A rise with no change in production points at the lining, the casing or the burners.
  • Zinc level and zinc added. A level that falls faster than production explains is a leak until proved otherwise.
  • Dross removed, each week and per tonne. A rise with no change in the work or the flux can mean the wall is dissolving faster.
  • Leak pit thermocouples. A rising reading means zinc in the pit.

At each shutdown, or when the kettle is emptied

  • Measure the wall thickness with an ultrasonic gauge on a fixed grid of points, closer together in the zones that run hot. The same points every time make the trend clear.
  • Measure the bulge of each long wall from a straight line at several heights.
  • Inspect the welds, the corners and the rim.
  • Inspect the furnace lining, baffles and burner blocks while the furnace is open.

Working out the remaining life

For example, a wall that started at 50 mm and measures 44 mm at its thinnest point after three years is losing 2 mm a year there. If the kettle maker's retirement thickness is 30 mm, there are about seven years left at that rate. If the same point read 47 mm a year earlier, the rate has risen to 3 mm a year and less than five years are left. The trend matters more than any single reading.

Warning signs

SignWhat it can meanAction
One wall zone runs hotter than the othersBurner jet, baffle or lining problem; local wearCheck burner alignment, baffles and lining; limit firing in that zone
Dross per tonne risingMore iron from the flux, the steel or the wallCheck flux iron first, then the wall temperatures
Fuel per tonne risingLining damage, casing leaks, burner tuningInspect lining and casing; tune the burners
Zinc level falling without explanationLeakCheck the leak pits at once; get the emergency plan ready
Leak pit thermocouple risingZinc in the leak pitStop firing that zone; follow the emergency plan
Bulge larger than at the last checkCreep of a hot or thin wallMeasure, lower the heat flux, plan the change
Zinc seen in the furnace or the flueWall breachEmergency stop

Plan the change

  • Order the new kettle well before the old one reaches its retirement thickness. Kettle steel and fabrication take time.
  • Plan the zinc transfer: pumping the zinc into moulds or a holding vessel takes equipment and time, and a zinc pump sized for the job makes it safer and quicker.
  • Reline and inspect the furnace while the kettle is out, and check the burners, baffles and thermocouples. See installing a new kettle.
  • Write the emergency plan for a kettle leak before you need it: who stops what, where the zinc goes, and how water is kept away.
