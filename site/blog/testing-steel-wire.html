Wire quality

Testing steel wire: tensile, torsion, reverse bend and wrapping

What the four standard wire tests measure, how each one is done, and what a failed test points to: the rod, the die sequence, heat in drawing or the annealing.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

A wire that is the right size can still crack in the customer's machine. Mechanical tests on samples from each coil or lot show whether it is strong and ductile enough for its use. Four simple tests cover most steel wire, and the way a sample fails tells you as much as the number it reaches.

TestISOIndian StandardMeasures
TensileISO 6892-1IS 1608 (Part 1)Strength and elongation
Simple torsionISO 7800IS 1717Twists to fracture; surface and internal soundness
Reverse bendISO 7801IS 1716Bends to fracture; ductility
WrappingISO 7802IS 1755Cracking when wound round a mandrel; coating adherence

These standards set out how to do each test. The values the wire has to reach are in the product standard and the order.

Tensile test

A length of wire is pulled to fracture. The maximum force divided by the original cross-section gives the tensile strength, and the stretch over a gauge length after fracture gives the elongation. Elongation depends on the gauge length used, so compare results only on the same gauge length. A break in or at the grips is not a valid test.

Strength rises with every pass as the wire work-hardens, so it follows the total reduction since the rod or the last anneal. Strength that varies from coil to coil at the same sizes points at the rod chemistry or at uneven annealing. Low elongation with high strength means the wire has had too much reduction, or has aged.

Simple torsion test

A length of wire is gripped at both ends and twisted about its own axis in one direction until it breaks, and the turns are counted. The fracture matters as much as the count. A clean, flat break square to the wire axis is normal. A split running along the wire, or a stepped, spiral fracture, means delamination or a seam. In high-carbon wire, delamination is the classic sign of strain ageing from heat in drawing.

Reverse bend test

The wire is clamped and bent through 90° one way and then the other, each time over a cylindrical support of a set radius, until it breaks. The number of bends measures ductility and is sensitive to surface defects, which is why it is used on spring, high-carbon and prestressing wire.

Wrapping test

The wire is wound in close turns round a mandrel, whose diameter the product standard sets as a multiple of the wire size, and the turns are inspected for cracks. On coated wire the same test checks the coating: zinc that cracks and can be rubbed off has poor adherence.

Sampling and records

  • Cut samples from the end of each coil, and from both ends where the specification asks for it.
  • Label every sample with the heat number, the machine, the die set and the date.
  • Keep results by heat and by machine. A trend on one machine points at its die sequence or cooling; a trend on one heat points at the rod.

ResultLook at
Low strengthTotal reduction, rod grade, over-annealing
High strength, low elongationToo much reduction since the last heat treatment; ageing
