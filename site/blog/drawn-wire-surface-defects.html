Wire quality

Surface defects on drawn wire: scratches, seams, bursts and dark marks

Scratches, seams, slivers, die marks and internal bursts each start in a different place. How to read them, tell rod from line defects and fix the cause.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

A surface defect on drawn wire is evidence. Some defects arrive with the rod from the rolling mill, and no drawing practice will remove them. Others are made on the line, at one die, one block or one guide. The first job is to tell the two apart, because the fix is in different hands.

Rod defect or line defect?

  • Rod defects run the full length of a coil in a steady pattern, are there from the first metre, and follow certain heats or one supplier.
  • Line defects appear or change at a moment you can find: after a die change, a speed change or a new batch of soap, or at one block of the machine. Stop the line, take a sample after each die, and the defect appears at the die that makes it.

Defects from the rod

  • Seams and laps. Folds of metal rolled into the surface at the mill. Drawing stretches them into fine, continuous lines but cannot weld them shut, and they open in torsion and bend tests and in the customer's forming.
  • Slivers. Thin flaps of metal rolled onto the surface, which can tear off during drawing and score the die.
  • Scale pits. Scale rolled into the rod surface leaves pits after descaling.
  • Inclusions. Non-metallic particles in the steel. Near the surface they show as defects; inside, they cause flat, brittle breaks. See wire breaks.

Keep the heat number with every coil, cut samples of suspect rod, and send them back with the complaint.

Defects made on the line

  • Longitudinal scratches. A chipped or cracked die, a hard particle caught at the die entry, a worn guide or dirty soap. A scratch at the same position round the wire on every length points at the die.
  • Periodic marks. Marks that repeat at a fixed spacing come from something that turns. Measure the spacing and compare it with the circumference of each block, roller and pulley on the wire path: marks every 1.88 m on a 600 mm block point straight at that block.
  • Bright bare stripes. The lubricant film has broken. Die wear and scratching follow.
  • Dark, burnt soap marks. The lubricant has overheated: the speed is too high for the cooling, or one pass takes too much. See heat in wire drawing.
  • Flats. Wire slipping on a block, or pressed by a damaged guide.
  • Central bursts. Arrow-shaped cracks inside the wire, invisible from outside. They come from light reductions through steep dies, where the delta factor runs above about 3. They show up in breaks with a cup at the centre and in sections cut and etched. See die angle and reduction.

Surface and the next process

Wire going on to galvanizing, electroplating or copper coating needs a clean, sound surface. Burnt soap and heavy lubricant residue cause bare spots in the zinc, and seams trap cleaning liquids. A defect a nail maker would never notice can fail a galvanized or welding wire.
