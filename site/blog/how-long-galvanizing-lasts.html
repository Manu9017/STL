Coating quality

How long will galvanizing last? Corrosivity, thickness and coating life

Coating life is zinc thickness divided by how fast zinc corrodes on site. How to estimate it from the ISO 9223 categories, with examples, and what changes it.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

Buyers ask how long a galvanized structure will last before it needs attention. Once two numbers are known, the answer is simple arithmetic: how much zinc is on the steel, and how fast zinc corrodes where the steel will stand. A tower by the sea and a shed in a dry inland town can carry exactly the same coating and get very different lives.

The arithmetic

Zinc corrodes slowly and fairly evenly. Its surface turns into a thin, dense layer of zinc carbonate, the patina, which slows the attack down. Until most of the zinc is used up the steel underneath does not rust, because zinc protects it sacrificially, even at small scratches. So the life of the coating is close to:

life in years ≈ coating thickness in µm ÷ zinc corrosion rate in µm a year

The thickness comes from the order and the inspection. The corrosion rate comes from the site.

Corrosivity categories

ISO 9223 sorts atmospheres into six categories by how fast standard metals corrode in their first year outdoors. For zinc the bands are:

CategoryCorrosivityZinc lost in the first yearTypical settings
C1Very lowup to 0.1 µmHeated, dry interiors
C2Low0.1 to 0.7 µmDry rural air; unheated buildings where condensation is rare
C3Medium0.7 to 2.1 µmTown and light industrial air; coast with little salt
C4High2.1 to 4.2 µmIndustrial areas; coast with moderate salt
C5Very high4.2 to 8.4 µmIndustry with high humidity; coast with heavy salt
CXExtreme8.4 to 25 µmOffshore, and the harshest industrial and tropical sites

The bands are wide on purpose: two sites in the same category can differ threefold. Where it matters, the category can be found by exposing zinc and steel samples on site for a year, or estimated from data on humidity, sulphur dioxide and salt deposition, both of which ISO 9223 describes.

These use first-year rates. Zinc usually corrodes more slowly after the first year, once the patina has formed, so real lives tend to fall towards the longer end. Plan the first maintenance before the zinc is gone: when the thinnest areas start to show rust, a zinc-rich paint or a paint system restores the protection at a fraction of the cost of new steel.

What changes the answer on a real structure

  • Wetness. Surfaces that stay damp, such as crevices, ledges and the insides of open sections, corrode faster than surfaces that dry quickly.
  • Shelter from rain. Rain washes salt and dust away. Near the sea or a chimney, sheltered surfaces can corrode faster than the exposed ones above them.
  • Local sources. Acid fume from a pickling shop, ammonia, fertiliser dust or a nearby chimney can put one corner of a site in a higher category than the rest.
  • Contact. Steel in soil, in concrete, in water or touching copper does not follow the atmospheric categories at all. Those cases need their own assessment.
  • The thickness actually supplied. Coatings on reactive steel are often well above the minimum and last longer in proportion. The minimum is a floor, not the expected value.

Getting more life

  • Ask for a thicker coating. Blast-cleaning the steel before galvanizing roughens it and gives a thicker coating. Agree the figure in the order.
  • Paint over the zinc. Galvanizing and paint together last much longer than either alone. See painting over galvanizing.
  • Design out water traps. Drain holes, open sections and sloping tops keep surfaces dry. See designing steel for galvanizing.
  • Inspect the worst places first. Joints, bases and sheltered faces show trouble long before open surfaces.
Note

A life estimate is only as good as the category chosen for the site. On long-life structures, measure the coating as supplied and record it with the site category, so the next inspection has a baseline to compare with.
