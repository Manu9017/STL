Galvanizing practice

Distortion in galvanizing: why steel moves in the kettle

A part that went in straight and came out bowed was usually set up to move before it met the zinc. Locked-in stress, uneven heating, and designing around them.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 2 min read

Some fabrications come out of the kettle bowed, twisted or buckled. The zinc is rarely to blame. At galvanizing temperature steel is softer than at room temperature, and any stress locked into the piece, or created by heating it unevenly, gets its chance to move it. Most distortion can be predicted from the drawing, and most of it can be designed out.

What happens at 450 °C

At around 450 °C structural steel is roughly a third less stiff than at room temperature, and it stops behaving elastically at well under half of its normal yield stress. Two kinds of stress then have their way:

  • Stress already in the steel. Rolling, cold forming, flame cutting and above all welding leave stresses locked in. When the steel softens they relax, and the piece moves to a new shape.
  • Stress from uneven heating. The part of the work already in the zinc expands while the part still in the air does not. Thin sections heat fast, thick ones slowly. The difference pulls the piece out of shape, and if the stress passes the softened yield point the change is permanent. Uneven cooling in the quench does the same in reverse.

Designs that tend to move

  • Thin plate welded to a heavy frame, and large flat panels, which buckle or oil-can.
  • Asymmetric sections and welds on one side only. Channels, angles and members with a heavy weld along one edge bow towards the weld.
  • Long, slender members hung horizontally, which sag under their own weight while they are hot.
  • Very different thicknesses in one piece, such as thin gussets on heavy beams.
  • Pieces too long for the kettle and dipped twice. Half the piece is at 450 °C while the other half is cold: the largest temperature difference any work ever sees.

Design it out

MeasureWhy it works
Symmetrical sections and balanced weldsLocked-in stresses balance each other as they relax
A welding sequence planned for even heat inputLess locked-in stress in the first place
Similar thicknesses in one fabrication; thin parts bolted on after galvanizingEven heating and cooling
Stiffeners on large flat panelsPanels hold their shape while soft
Temporary bracing across open framesHolds the geometry through the dip
Pieces sized to fit in one dipNo half-hot, half-cold stage
Vent and drain holes of the right sizeFaster, smoother immersion and withdrawal

For kettle sizes, see sizing a galvanizing kettle.

What the galvanizer can do

  • Dip steadily and briskly. Lower the work as fast as the bath allows without splashing, so the whole piece spends less time half in and half out. See dipping practice.
  • Warm the work first. Dried work at 60 to 120 °C meets a smaller temperature jump than cold steel.
  • Hang long pieces well. Enough support points, at an angle that lets zinc drain without leaving long spans unsupported while hot.
  • Cool it gently. Thin, distortion-prone work can be left to cool in air instead of being quenched. Agree it with the customer.
  • Straighten carefully. Moderate bows can be pressed out cold after galvanizing; check the coating afterwards.
Note

Send the drawing before fabrication. A balanced weld or a bolted gusset costs nothing at the drawing stage and saves a rejected piece later.
