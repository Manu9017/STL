Coating quality

Silicon in steel and the Sandelin effect

Why some steel comes out of the zinc thick, grey and brittle, and what galvanizers and their customers can do about it.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited24 September 2026 · 2 min read

Two pieces of steel, the same thickness, dipped in the same kettle for the same time: one comes out bright with a coating of 90 µm, the other dull grey with 200 µm that flakes when knocked. The difference is almost always silicon, and sometimes phosphorus, in the steel.

How the coating grows

A galvanized coating is not a layer of zinc stuck to the steel. It is a series of zinc-iron alloy layers that grow by reaction, with a layer of nearly pure zinc on top. On ordinary steel the alloy layers slow their own growth, so the coating thickness levels off. Silicon disturbs this: in certain ranges it makes one of the alloy layers loose and fast-growing, so the reaction keeps going for as long as the steel is in the bath.

Silicon in steelBehaviourTypical result
Up to about 0.03 %Normal, self-limitingBright, even coating
About 0.03 to 0.12 % (Sandelin range)Very reactiveThick, grey, can be brittle
About 0.15 to 0.25 % (Sebisty range)Moderately reactiveThicker than normal, usually acceptable
Above about 0.28 %Reactive, rising with siliconThick, grey

Phosphorus adds to the effect; a common rule of thumb looks at silicon plus about 2.5 times phosphorus. The exact boundaries vary with bath temperature and alloy additions, so treat the ranges as a guide.

Why it matters commercially

  • Reactive steel can take two times the zinc of normal steel for the same area, which goes straight into your zinc bill.
  • Thick, grey coatings attract customer complaints even though they protect well.
  • Very thick coatings are more brittle and can flake at edges during handling.

What the galvanizer can do

  • Keep immersion as short as the steel allows; reactive steel keeps growing coating the longer it stays in.
  • Run the bath at the lower end of the normal range, around 445 °C, for reactive work.
  • Use bath alloying; small nickel additions reduce reactivity in the Sandelin range.
  • Separate reactive steel and dip it with its own settings rather than mixing it with normal work.
  • Quench promptly to stop alloy growth after withdrawal.

What the customer can do

The best solution is upstream. When ordering steel that will be galvanized, specify silicon either at or below about 0.03 per cent, or in the 0.15 to 0.25 per cent window, and ask for the mill certificate. Solar, tower and crash-barrier makers who buy large tonnages of the same grades can make a big difference to their galvanizing cost this way.
