Galvanizing practice

After the kettle: quench, passivation and inspection

The last three steps decide how the work looks at the customer's site and whether it passes inspection.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited4 November 2026 · 2 min read

Quench

Quenching in water cools the work for handling and stops the zinc–iron reaction, which keeps coatings from growing thicker and greyer on heavy steel. Keep the quench water clean and cool, and change it before it becomes heavily loaded with salts. Not every part should be quenched: thin parts likely to distort, and some high-strength steels, may need to cool in air; agree this with the customer.

Note

The quench tank gets hot from the work. In our plants that heat warms the flux tank, which saves fuel on both.

Passivation

A passivating dip forms a thin conversion film that slows white rust in the first weeks, while work is stored and transported. Trivalent chromium and chromium-free passivates have largely replaced hexavalent chromium. If the work will be painted later, check with the paint supplier: some passivates must be left out.

Inspection

  • Look at every piece: continuous coating, no bare spots, no lumps that affect use, no flux residues or ash.
  • Measure thickness with a magnetic gauge at several places on each sampled piece.
  • Remove spikes and sharp drips; check holes and threads are clear.
  • Record results against the order's standard.
Steel thicknessLocal minimumMean minimum
Over 6 mm70 µm85 µm
3 to 6 mm55 µm70 µm
1.5 to 3 mm45 µm55 µm
Under 1.5 mm35 µm45 µm

Those are the ISO 1461 minimums for steel articles that are not centrifuged; IS 4759 follows the same approach. Check the edition and any project specification on the order.

Storage and dispatch

Stack work with spacers, off the ground and sloped to drain, under cover where possible. Dispatch dry. These simple habits prevent most white rust complaints.
