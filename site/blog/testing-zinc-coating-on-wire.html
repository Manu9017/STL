Wire quality

Testing the zinc on galvanized wire: coating mass, uniformity and adherence

How galvanized wire coatings are tested: strip and weigh, with the formula and a worked example, the Preece dip for uniformity, and wrapping for adherence.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 2 min read

Galvanized wire is bought for its zinc. Fencing, barbed wire, mesh and armouring last as long as the coating does, and the coating mass is the number on the contract. Three tests check it: how much zinc there is, whether it is even, and whether it stays on when the wire is bent.

Coating mass: strip and weigh

The standard method, described in IS 6745, ISO 7989-2 and ASTM A90, dissolves the zinc and weighs what is left:

  1. Cut a sample of known length, clean it with a solvent and dry it.
  2. Weigh it: W1.
  3. Dissolve the zinc in inhibited hydrochloric acid until the bubbling stops. The inhibitor stops the acid attacking the steel once the zinc has gone.
  4. Rinse, dry and weigh again: W2. Measure the stripped diameter, d, in mm.
  5. Coating mass in g/m² = (W1 − W2) ÷ W2 × d × 1960.

The constant 1960 is the density of steel, 7,850 kg/m³, divided by four, with the units worked in. For example, a 300 mm sample of 2.5 mm wire weighs 12.13 g before stripping and 11.56 g after: (12.13 − 11.56) ÷ 11.56 × 2.5 × 1960 = 242 g/m².

Uniformity: the Preece test

The Preece test, set out in IS 2633, dips the sample in a copper sulphate solution for one minute at a time, washing and wiping it between dips. Where the zinc has been dissolved through, copper plates out on the bare steel as a bright, adherent red deposit. The number of dips the wire survives without that deposit is compared with the product standard. Copper at the cut ends does not count. Thin spots and bare patches show up quickly in this test, where a mass test, averaging over the whole sample, can miss them.

Adherence: the wrapping test

The wire is wound round a mandrel of a set diameter and the coating is inspected. It should not crack or flake so that it can be rubbed off with bare fingers. Poor adherence comes from a thick, brittle alloy layer, from too long in the zinc or too hot a bath, or from zinc laid over a surface that was not properly cleaned.

What sets the coating on the line

  • Line speed and wiping. A faster line pulls out more zinc; harder wiping takes more off. Together they set the coating mass.
  • Wire size. Thicker wire carries more heat and more zinc, and coating classes in standards such as IS 4826 and ASTM A641 rise with wire diameter.
  • Bath temperature. A steady bath gives a steady coat.
  • Cleaning. Bare spots on wire almost always start in cleaning or fluxing, not in the zinc. See galvanized wire.

The cost of over-coating

Coating above the class ordered is zinc given away. On 2.5 mm wire, with about 204 m² of surface per tonne, every 10 g/m² of extra coating is 2 kg of zinc per tonne of wire.
