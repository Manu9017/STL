Coating quality

Painting over galvanizing: duplex systems that stay on

Paint on fresh zinc often peels within a year. Why it happens, how to prepare the surface, which paints work, and why zinc plus paint outlasts either one alone.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

A duplex system is hot-dip galvanizing with paint or powder coating on top. It is chosen for colour, for safety marking, for very corrosive sites, or simply for a longer life. Done properly it is one of the most durable ways to protect steel. Done carelessly, the paint peels off in sheets within months, and the galvanizing gets the blame.

Why paint peels from zinc

  • A smooth, fresh surface. Newly galvanized zinc is smooth and gives paint little to grip.
  • The wrong paint. Alkyd paints, the common oil-based primers, react with zinc. The alkaline zinc surface turns the binder into soap at the interface and the paint lets go. This is called saponification, and it is the classic cause of paint failure on galvanizing.
  • A film in between. Passivation films, oil, salts from the quench water and white rust all sit between the zinc and the paint.
  • Damp. Paint applied below the dew point, or over a surface that is not dry, blisters and lifts.

Tell the galvanizer before the dip

Work that will be painted should be ordered that way. Ask for it to be left without passivation, or agree a passivate that the paint maker accepts, because conversion films can spoil adhesion. Ask for spikes, drips and ash to be removed: they stand proud of the paint film and are the first places to rust. Then store the work dry and paint it soon. ASTM D6386 treats newly galvanized, partly weathered and fully weathered zinc as three different surfaces, each with its own preparation.

Preparing the surface

  1. Clean. Wash off oil and dirt with an alkaline cleaner, rinse and dry.
  2. Give the paint a key. A light sweep blast with a fine, non-metallic abrasive at low pressure roughens the zinc without taking much of it off. Where blasting is not possible, a conversion treatment or an etch primer made for zinc does the same job.
  3. Paint promptly, before the surface picks up salts or white rust, with the steel at least 3 °C above the dew point.

Powder coating needs one more step. Galvanized steel can release gas in the curing oven, which leaves pinholes in the powder. A pre-heat that drives the gas out before powdering, and a powder made for galvanized steel, prevent it. ASTM D7803 covers this preparation.

Choosing the paint

Use a primer that the paint maker states is suitable for hot-dip galvanized steel, never a plain alkyd. Two-pack epoxy primers with polyurethane finishes, and suitable acrylics, are common choices. ISO 12944-5 lists paint systems for galvanized surfaces by corrosivity category and life, so the system can be matched to the site.

Why duplex lasts longer than the sum of its parts

The paint shields the zinc from the weather, so the zinc corrodes far more slowly. The zinc stops rust creeping under the paint from a scratch, so the paint does not blister and flake outwards from the damage. The usual estimate of a duplex system's life is 1.5 to 2.3 times the sum of the two lives on their own, the lower factor for the harshest marine and industrial sites. A coating good for 40 years and a paint good for 10 are therefore estimated at 75 to 115 years together. In practice the paint is renewed long before that, and the zinc underneath makes every repaint simpler, because the steel has not rusted under the old film.

Paint peeling in sheets, shiny zinc underneathNo key on smooth zinc, or a passivation filmSweep blast or etch; order without passivation

Pinholes in powder coatingGas leaving the coating in the ovenPre-heat to degas; a powder made for galvanizing
