Embrittlement and galvanizing: cold work, high-strength steel and bolts

Brittle cracking after galvanizing is rare and nearly always predictable. Strain ageing, hydrogen, high-strength bolts and liquid metal cracking, and the cures.

KVKrishan VermaCEO, SHIVAS Technology Limited22 November 2026 · 3 min read

Ordinary structural steel comes out of the zinc with the strength and toughness it went in with. Brittle cracking after galvanizing is rare. When it does happen it is serious, because a member can crack while it is handled or erected. There are four mechanisms, and each shows its warning signs on the drawing, long before the steel reaches the kettle.

1. Strain-age embrittlement

Cold work, such as bending to a tight radius, punching holes or shearing thick plate, strains the steel. Heated afterwards to around 450 °C, strained steel ages: carbon and nitrogen atoms lock the deformed structure, and the steel loses ductility and toughness. The heat of galvanizing does in minutes what would otherwise take years. Thick sections, heavy cold work, notches and steels high in free nitrogen are most at risk. Aluminium-killed steels, in which aluminium ties up the nitrogen, are far less susceptible.

  • ASTM A143 recommends a cold bend radius of at least three times the section thickness for work that will be galvanized.
  • Bend tighter radii hot, or stress relieve heavily cold-worked parts before galvanizing. ASTM A143 limits that stress relief to 595 °C for sections that were bent cold.
  • Holes punched in thick plate leave a cold-worked rim. Drill them, or punch them undersize and ream the worked metal away.
  • Grind the sheared edges of thick plate that will carry tension.

2. Hydrogen embrittlement

Pickling in acid produces hydrogen at the steel surface, and some of it enters the steel. In ordinary steels it escapes again when the work is heated in the zinc. Very high-strength steels, above about 1,100 MPa tensile strength, have a fine structure that traps hydrogen, and the coating then slows its escape, so the steel can crack later under load. For these steels, blast-clean instead of pickling, followed by a flash pickle of seconds only to remove the blasting residue. Inhibited acid and short pickling times help in every case.

3. High-strength bolts

ISO 10684 covers hot-dip galvanized coarse-thread fasteners from M8 to M64, for bolts up to property class 10.9 and nuts up to class 12. Smaller threads are not recommended for hot-dip galvanizing. For class 10.9 bolts the hydrogen risk is managed with blast cleaning instead of long pickling, a controlled process and testing. Class 12.9 bolts are outside ISO 10684; ask the bolt maker before galvanizing anything above 10.9. Nuts are tapped oversize after galvanizing so they run on the coated bolt, and the zinc on the bolt protects the bare thread of the nut. See spin galvanizing for fasteners.

4. Liquid metal assisted cracking

In rare cases a heavily welded, highly restrained fabrication cracks in the zinc itself. Molten zinc penetrates the grain boundaries where high tensile stress, from welding and from uneven heating during the dip, meets a susceptible steel. High-strength steels, thick welds in rigid joints, hard flame-cut edges and notches raise the risk. The German DASt guideline 022 deals with it for load-bearing steelwork. The defences are in design and fabrication: reduce restraint, avoid notches, grind hard edges, balance welds, and inspect critical members after galvanizing, for example by magnetic particle testing.
